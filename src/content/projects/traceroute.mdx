A cyber-forensics investigator is called to an apartment where a man has been found dead. The machine records say one
thing; the body says another. You tap through the rooms collecting evidence, read the victim's laptop, question three
suspects, and use a handheld device to sort out threads, statements and a timeline until the manipulated part of the
record gives itself away.

It was built for a university mobile development course by three of us in about three weeks, with a hard rule from
the start: **Java and Android only. No Unity, no Unreal, no Kotlin.** My two teammates are strong programmers who
don't come from games, so a lot of my job was turning game-shaped problems into structure they could build on. I wrote the renderer and almost all of the game code and drew most of the art; the XML UI was shared between the three of us.

## Architecture

I set up the base: the module layout and the first forty-odd classes, so all three of us were filling in agreed
interfaces instead of inventing structure in parallel. `MainActivity` stays thin and hosts a shell fragment; the world
is a `GLSurfaceView`, and the handheld, laptop, dialogue and completion screens are ordinary Android views layered on
top as fragments.

A single `GameRuntime` owns the gameplay systems: evidence, investigation threads, timeline, suspect statements and a
guidance system that works out the next lead. The case itself is authored data in a JSON file, so collecting a piece of
evidence updates evidence state, which updates threads, which updates the timeline and statements, without any of that
being hard-coded per clue. Progress is saved to SQLite as a compact key/value table.

## Art

The apartment rooms are hand-drawn. I drew most of them on my iPad: flat, inked illustrations with hard pools of
light, so the post-process could push the noir mood without fighting the drawing.

## Rendering

The rooms are drawn through a small OpenGL ES 3.0 renderer I wrote for it: shader programs, textures, textured quads,
a camera and a render pipeline, starting from the first commit where a shader filled the screen with a solid colour.
Touch input arrives as Android `MotionEvent`s, is normalised to 0–1 screen space and queued onto the GL thread, where
the hotspot system maps it to evidence, navigation, the laptop or the handheld.

The room art goes through a post-process pass that only touches the GL view, never the Android UI on top: exposure and
an ACES-approximation tonemapper, a vignette, optional ordered dithering (4×4 or 8×8 Bayer, or hash noise) with a
three-tone palette, and optional scanlines and CRT warp. Every value lives in one settings class so the look can be
tuned in one place.

## Systems

I built the evidence, thread, statement and final-timeline systems, the handheld and terminal mechanics, the guidance
system and visual annotations, audio cues, and the persistence for case completion. JUnit tests cover progression,
content validation, hotspot behaviour, settings and save state.

We planned and tracked the whole thing on a Miro board, which kept a three-week, three-person project from turning
into three separate games.
